## Results

### Bivariate Correlations (1,879 weeks)

| Pair | Pearson r | p-value | Spearman ρ |
|------|-----------|---------|------------|
| Friction vs Compliance | 0.1099 | 0.000002 | 0.6067 |
| Friction vs Political Activity | -0.0477 | 0.039 | -0.0341 |
| Compliance vs Political Activity | 0.0038 | 0.868 | -0.0257 |

Neither friction nor compliance is meaningfully correlated with the
political activity index. Friction has a weak *negative* correlation
with political activity (r = -0.048), suggesting friction events may
slightly favor recess periods rather than session periods.

### Partial Correlation

**Interpretation:** The political activity index is not a confounding

### Session vs Recess Breakdown

| Period | Weeks | Pearson r | p-value | Spearman ρ |
|--------|-------|-----------|---------|------------|
| In session (activity ≥ 0.7) | 1,226 | 0.1625 | < 0.0001 | 0.5707 |

**Interesting finding:** The Pearson correlation is slightly stronger
during session periods (r = 0.163) than recess (r = 0.093), but the
Spearman rank correlation is actually *stronger* during recess (ρ = 0.670
vs 0.571). This suggests the rank-order pattern persists regardless of
congressional activity levels.

## Does This Change Prior Conclusions?

**No — it strengthens them.** The prior concern was that friction and
compliance might co-move simply because both increase during busy
political periods. This test shows they do NOT correlate with the
congressional calendar, and the friction-compliance correlation is
completely independent of political activity levels.
